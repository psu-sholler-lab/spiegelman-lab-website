Current projects

Research Overview

The Spiegelman Laboratory investigates the molecular mechanisms that drive cancer initiation, progression, metastasis, and therapeutic resistance. Our research focuses on how RNA-binding proteins, tumor–microenvironment interactions, extracellular vesicles, and immune regulation shape malignant behavior. We combine molecular and cellular biology with patient-derived models, organoids, genetically engineered systems, animal models, proteomics, and functional genomic screening to identify mechanisms that can be translated into new diagnostic and therapeutic strategies.

RNA-Binding Proteins and Cancer

A central focus of our laboratory is the role of RNA-binding proteins in cancer biology, particularly insulin-like growth factor 2 mRNA-binding protein 1 (IGF2BP1), also known as CRD-BP or IMP1. IGF2BP1 regulates gene expression after transcription by binding specific messenger RNAs and controlling their stability, localization, and translation. Our studies investigate how increased IGF2BP1 expression promotes tumor-cell proliferation, survival, invasion, stem-like properties, metastasis, and resistance to therapy in cancers including neuroblastoma, colorectal cancer, melanoma, and lung carcinoma. We are also defining the RNA targets, protein partners, and signaling pathways that enable IGF2BP1 to function as an oncogenic regulator. An important translational objective is to develop and evaluate selective IGF2BP1 inhibitors and determine whether targeting this protein can suppress tumor growth, limit metastatic disease, and improve responses to existing treatments.

Neuroblastoma Metastasis

A major area of laboratory research is high-risk neuroblastoma, an aggressive pediatric cancer in which metastatic disease remains a leading cause of treatment failure. We investigate the molecular mechanisms that allow neuroblastoma cells to disseminate, survive in distant tissues, and establish metastatic tumors. Our studies have identified IGF2BP1 as an important regulator of neuroblastoma progression and are examining how it influences tumor-cell behavior, metastatic fitness, and interactions with the surrounding tissue. We use complementary models—including highly metastatic mouse cell lines developed in our lab, patient-derived systems, organoids, immunocompetent mouse models, and specialized models of neuroblastoma—to study both tumor-intrinsic mechanisms and tumor–host interactions. These efforts are directed toward identifying vulnerabilities that can be targeted to prevent or treat metastatic neuroblastoma.

Extracellular Vesicles and the Tumor Microenvironment

We investigate how extracellular vesicles, including exosomes, mediate communication between tumor cells and distant tissues. These vesicles transport proteins, RNAs, and other biological signals that can remodel the tumor microenvironment and prepare distant organs for metastatic colonization. Our research has examined how extracellular-vesicle cargo is regulated and how vesicle-associated factors contribute to the formation of pre-metastatic niches in neuroblastoma and other cancers. By defining the molecular contents and biological effects of cancer-associated vesicles, we seek to develop new biomarkers and therapeutic strategies that disrupt tumor communication.

Cancer Immunology and Therapy Resistance

Another important research interest is the relationship between oncogenic RNA regulation, the immune microenvironment, and response to cancer therapy. We study how tumor cells use molecular signals—including RNA-binding proteins and extracellular-vesicle cargo—to suppress antitumor immunity and promote resistance to treatment. In neuroblastoma, our work examines mechanisms that limit the effectiveness of anti-GD2 immunotherapy and investigates strategies for improving immune-mediated tumor control. We are particularly interested in how the tumor microenvironment influences innate and adaptive immune responses, and how these interactions can be therapeutically modified. Our long-term goal is to develop rational combination treatments that integrate targeted inhibitors, chemotherapy, and immunotherapy to overcome resistance and improve outcomes for patients with high-risk disease.

Melanoma Metastasis and Therapeutic Vulnerabilities

The laboratory also studies the molecular basis of melanoma development and metastatic progression. Our earlier work established a role for CRD-BP/IGF2BP1 in malignant melanoma, while subsequent studies examined how RNA-binding proteins and extracellular vesicles influence melanoma-cell behavior and communication with distant tissues. We use in vitro and in vivo models, together with functional genomic approaches such as CRISPR screening, to identify genes that are required for metastatic growth. These studies have revealed potential vulnerabilities, including Polr1a-dependent mechanisms that may be targeted therapeutically. By integrating molecular biology, animal models, and genomic screening, we aim to identify actionable drivers of melanoma metastasis and develop strategies to prevent the spread of disease.

Translational Models and Therapeutic Development

Across all our research programs, we pursue a translational approach that connects mechanistic discoveries to clinically relevant models and therapeutic development. We use patient-derived organoids, immunocompetent animal models, metastatic cell lines, extracellular-vesicle platforms, proteomic analyses, and CRISPR-based functional screens to study cancer in biologically complex settings. These systems allow us to examine tumor growth, metastasis, immune interactions, and treatment response more accurately than simplified experimental models alone. Our overarching goal is to define the molecular mechanisms that make cancer aggressive and treatment-resistant, and then translate those discoveries into targeted therapies, improved immunotherapy combinations, and precision-medicine approaches for pediatric and adult cancers.
